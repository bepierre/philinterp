Mechanistic Indicators of Understanding in Large Language Models

Pierre Beckmann and Matthieu Queloz, Philosophical Studies, 2026

Language models are often said to imitate patterns without understanding anything. The paper argues that mechanistic findings make this picture hard to hold, once they are read through a theory of what understanding is. It proposes three tiers, each tied to a level of the model's organisation: conceptual understanding, when a model forms features as directions in latent space; state-of-the-world understanding, when it learns contingent connections between features and tracks how the world changes; and principled understanding, when it stops relying on memorised facts and finds a compact circuit that connects them.

World Modeling in Transformers

Pierre Beckmann, Matthieu Queloz and André Freitas, 2026

TaxiGPT is a transformer trained on random walks through Manhattan. Its wrong turns had been read as proof that it holds no coherent map. Looking inside tells a different story: the model represents intersections and the streets between them, tracks its own position over a rolling window of recent moves, and steers by a goal compass, a circular representation of the bearing to its destination. Its failures come from interference between intersection features stored in superposition, not from the absence of a map.

Where is the Mind? Persona Vectors and LLM Individuation

Pierre Beckmann and Patrick Butlin, 2026

If a language model has a mind, which thing is it: the weights, a conversation, a character the model plays? The individuation problem asks which entities associated with a model should count as minds. The paper approaches it with mechanistic evidence, in particular recent work on persona vectors, persona space, and emergent misalignment, and narrows the field to three candidates: the virtual instance, sustained by attention streams that carry quasi-psychological connections across token-time; the instance-persona; and the model-persona.

Deep Learning Models Also Recall Features

Pierre Beckmann, 2026

Mechanistic interpretability has studied how language models recall facts stored in their weights. The paper argues that factual recall is an instance of something broader, feature recall: a linear projection can be read as retrieving stored information, scaled by how strongly an input feature is active. When Michael and Jordan are both present, an MLP combines them into a Michael Jordan feature; that single feature then recalls what the weights hold about him, plays basketball, Chicago Bulls, and adds it to the residual stream for the next prediction.

The paper defines feature recall, shows it applies across architectures, contrasts it with the established paradigm of feature combination, and asks how cases of it could be identified mechanistically.
