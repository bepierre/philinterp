Mechanistic Indicators of Understanding in Large Language Models

Pierre Beckmann and Matthieu Queloz, Philosophical Studies, 2026

Language models are often said to imitate patterns without understanding anything. The paper argues that mechanistic findings make this picture hard to hold, once they are read through a theory of what understanding is. It proposes three tiers, each tied to a level of the model's organisation: conceptual understanding, when a model forms features as directions in latent space; state-of-the-world understanding, when it learns contingent connections between features and tracks how the world changes; and principled understanding, when it stops relying on memorised facts and finds a compact circuit that connects them.

Intention-like Representations in Language Models?

Iwan Williams, Philosophical Studies, 2026

Philosophers and AI researchers increasingly posit internal representations in language models. How do these relate to the mental states we ascribe to each other? Belief-like states have had attention; intentions have had little. The paper takes five properties of intentions from the philosophical literature, directive function, distality, abstraction, commitment and planning, and tests two candidate classes of representations against them: function vectors, and the planning representations found in circuit-tracing work.

The result is mixed. These representations are intention-like in many, perhaps surprising, respects, yet differ from human intentions in important ways. Whether to call them intentions means weighing the costs and pay-offs of extending the concept beyond the human case.

World Modeling in Transformers

Pierre Beckmann, Matthieu Queloz and André Freitas, 2026

TaxiGPT is a transformer trained on random walks through Manhattan. Its wrong turns had been read as proof that it holds no coherent map. Looking inside tells a different story: the model represents intersections and the streets between them, tracks its own position over a rolling window of recent moves, and steers by a goal compass, a circular representation of the bearing to its destination. Its failures come from interference between intersection features stored in superposition, not from the absence of a map.

The interactive explorer lets you play a ride and watch the model's position code and goal compass update turn by turn.

Where is the Mind? Persona Vectors and LLM Individuation

Pierre Beckmann and Patrick Butlin, forthcoming in Ergo

If a language model has a mind, which thing is it: the weights, a conversation, a character the model plays? The individuation problem asks which entities associated with a model should count as minds. The paper approaches it with mechanistic evidence, in particular recent work on persona vectors, persona space, and emergent misalignment, and narrows the field to three candidates: the virtual instance, sustained by attention streams that carry quasi-psychological connections across token-time; the instance-persona; and the model-persona.

Probing Persona-Dependent Preferences in Language Models

Oscar Gilg, Pierre Beckmann, Daniel Paleka and Patrick Butlin, NeurIPS 2026

Language models can be said to have preferences: they reliably pick some tasks over others. But a model can also adopt personas with radically different preferences. Does each persona keep its own preference representations, or are some shared? The paper finds a preference vector: a direction that tracks the model's preferences across prompts and situations, and steering along it controls which task the model picks. Found on the helpful assistant, the same vector also predicts and steers the choices of an evil persona whose preferences are the reverse of the assistant's.

Recent research has shown that language models frequently enact personas. This paper indicates that doing so can rely on shared circuitry: representations that are common across personas and keep their meaning, while what is encoded along them changes with the persona currently active.

Mechanistic Interpretability Needs Philosophy

Iwan Williams, Ninell Oldenburg, Ruchira Dhar, Joshua Hatherley, Constanza Fierro, Nina Rajcic, Sandrine R. Schiller, Filippos Stamatiou and Anders Søgaard, NeurIPS 2026

Mechanistic interpretability aims to explain how neural networks work by uncovering their underlying mechanisms. As the field grows in influence, the paper argues, it matters to examine not just the models but the assumptions, concepts, and explanatory strategies implicit in the research: mechanistic interpretability needs philosophy as an ongoing partner in clarifying its concepts, refining its methods, and navigating the epistemic and ethical complexities of interpreting AI systems.

Three open problems make the case. How should a network be decomposed into interpretable parts? What are the features a model discovers and uses, and what do they represent? And how could deception be detected from a model's internals? In each, a philosophical literature already exists that the field can draw on.

Deep Learning Models Also Recall Features

Pierre Beckmann, 2026

Mechanistic interpretability has studied how language models recall facts stored in their weights. The paper argues that factual recall is an instance of something broader, feature recall: a linear projection can be read as retrieving stored information, scaled by how strongly an input feature is active. When Michael and Jordan are both present, an MLP combines them into a Michael Jordan feature; that single feature then recalls what the weights hold about him, plays basketball, Chicago Bulls, and adds it to the residual stream for the next prediction.

The paper defines feature recall, shows it applies across architectures, contrasts it with the established paradigm of feature combination, and asks how cases of it could be identified mechanistically.
